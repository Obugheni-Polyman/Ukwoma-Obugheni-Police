5 Signs Your Child Needs Learning Support, and What To Do Next

By Obugheni-Polyman Consulting Services · Abua/Odual, Rivers State · For parents, teachers and school leaders · 7-minute read

Every parent wants their child to do well in school. But what do you do when your child is trying hard, yet the results are not showing? When homework becomes a daily battle? When a child who was once happy to go to school now cries, pretends to be sick, or says "I hate school"?

You are not alone. In our work with families and schools in Abua/Odual, Buguma City and Port Harcourt, we meet many bright children who are not lazy, not stubborn and not dull. They simply learn differently, and they need the right support at the right time.

Learning support does not mean your child has failed. It means your child needs a different approach. The earlier you notice the signs, the easier it is to help.

Please note: these signs are a guide, not a diagnosis. Only qualified professionals can diagnose a learning difficulty or a medical condition.

Rule out these five things first

Before you worry about a learning difficulty, check the simple causes that look like one:

  • Eyesight: can your child see the board and read small print? A basic eye test at a clinic can solve many "reading problems".
  • Hearing: does your child often say "what?" or miss instructions? Ask for a hearing check.
  • Health and food: hunger, tiredness, malaria and poor sleep all reduce learning.
  • Language: many children in Rivers State speak Abua, Kalabari or another language at home, but are taught in English from the first day. Struggling with the language of the classroom is not the same as a learning difficulty.
  • Stress at home or school: a new school, a bereavement, bullying or conflict at home can show up as poor results.

If these are fine and the struggle continues, look at the five signs below.

1. Your child struggles with reading, writing or maths despite trying hard

This is the most common sign. Your child studies, you help at home and the teacher teaches in class, yet reading is still slow, spelling is still difficult or maths still feels confusing.

Examples to look for:

  • Reads very slowly, skips words, or guesses words instead of reading them
  • Keeps reversing letters like b and d, or writing numbers backwards, well after age 7 (some reversal before that age is normal)
  • Cannot remember multiplication tables even after many repetitions
  • Writes well when copying, but cannot write a simple sentence alone

What this may mean: your child may have a specific learning difficulty, or may simply need a different teaching method. It does not mean low intelligence. Many of these children are very capable in other areas, such as speaking, drawing, building or remembering stories.

What to do: do not just say "read more". Watch for two weeks and write down exactly what is difficult: reading, spelling, writing or maths. That record will help a teacher or assessor greatly.

2. Homework takes too long and ends in tears

If a 30-minute homework regularly takes two hours, with crying and shouting for both parent and child, something is wrong.

Children who need learning support often tire quickly because their brain works twice as hard to do the same task. It is like running a race with a heavy bag on your back.

What to do: break homework into small parts, for example 15 minutes of work and a 5-minute break. Use a quiet place with no TV. Praise effort, not only correct answers. If the struggle continues for more than three weeks, seek an assessment.

3. Your child avoids school or says they hate school

Children rarely hate learning itself. They hate feeling embarrassed, lost and different.

Watch for:

  • Frequent stomach aches or headaches on school mornings
  • Pretending to be sick to stay home
  • Saying "I am stupid" or "I cannot do it"
  • Becoming very quiet, or becoming very troublesome in class

Sometimes what looks like bad behaviour is frustration. A child who cannot read may disturb the class to avoid reading aloud. A child who is lost in maths may laugh and play instead of admitting it.

What to do: do not punish the avoidance first. Ask gently, "What part of school is hardest for you?" Listen without judging. Then speak to the teacher privately. If your child seems very sad, anxious or afraid, also speak to a school counselor or a health professional.

4. Your child forgets quickly what they just learned

You teach on Monday and they know it on Monday evening. By Wednesday it is gone. You teach again and they forget again.

This can point to weak working memory, or to a need for more practice in different ways. Some children learn by seeing, some by hearing and some by doing. If your child is taught in only one way, they may forget quickly.

What to do: change the method. Use pictures, songs, real objects and daily life. Teach counting with real naira notes, not only on paper. Teach spelling by building words with letter cards. If forgetting continues, an assessment can show your child's learning style and memory strengths.

5. There is a big gap between what your child says and what they write

Many parents miss this sign. Your child explains a story beautifully but cannot write two sentences about it. Or your child understands maths when you explain it aloud, but fails the written test.

This gap matters. It suggests the problem is not intelligence or understanding, but reading, writing or putting learning on paper.

What to do: let your child say the answer first, then write it with help. Allow oral answers at home. Ask the teacher whether your child can sometimes answer orally while you work on writing skills. This builds confidence while you deal with the root issue.

What to do next: 4 practical steps for parents

Step 1: Observe and write it down

For two weeks, keep a simple notebook with the date, subject, what was difficult, how long homework took and what your child said. This turns "my child is dull" into "here is what we have seen".

Step 2: Talk to the teacher

Ask for a private meeting. Ask, "Have you noticed this too? What support is available in school? Can my child sit nearer the front? Can we try extra time?" A good teacher welcomes a parent who comes with observations, not accusations.

Step 3: Get a learning needs assessment

An assessment is not a label to shame your child. It is a map to help your child. A learning needs assessment looks at reading, writing, maths, memory, attention and learning style. You receive a clear summary and practical strategies for home and school and, where needed, an Individualized Education Plan (IEP) with measurable goals. It describes how your child learns and what support helps. A medical or psychological diagnosis, where needed, comes from a qualified clinician.

Early support saves years of frustration. It is better to start at age 7 than to wait until 13, when the child may have lost confidence.

Step 4: Support at home without shame

Your words at home matter more than any textbook. Avoid "Why can't you be like your brother?" or "You are too dull." Say, "You learn differently, and we will find your way." Celebrate small wins, read together and keep a calm homework routine.

Frequently asked questions

Does learning support mean my child will always be behind?

No. With the right support, many children catch up. Early support makes the biggest difference.

Is this the same as special education?

Learning support is part of inclusive education: extra help so every child can learn in the same school. Special education is for children who need more intensive, ongoing support.

Will my child be labelled?

A good assessment does not label to shame. It explains in order to help. The report belongs to you as the parent, and you decide who sees it.

A final word for parents

If you recognised two or more of these signs, do not panic and do not wait. Your child is not a problem to be fixed. Your child is a learner to be understood. One assessment, one honest conversation with the teacher and one change in how you support at home can change your child's whole school story.
