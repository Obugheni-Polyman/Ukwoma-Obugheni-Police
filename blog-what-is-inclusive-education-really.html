What Is Inclusive Education Really? A Simple Guide for Nigerian Schools

By Obugheni-Polyman Consulting Services · Abua/Odual, Rivers State · For school owners, head teachers, teachers and parents · 8-minute read

Walk into many schools in Nigeria today and you will hear the phrase "inclusive education". It is in school brochures, government policies and training workshops. But what does it really mean?

Does it mean every child must sit in the same classroom? Does it mean one teacher must teach 60 children with different needs without help? Does it mean we should close special schools?

No. That is not inclusive education.

In our work with schools in Abua/Odual, Buguma City and Port Harcourt, we have seen both the confusion and the beauty of true inclusion. Done well, it changes not only the child with special needs, but the whole school. Done badly, it frustrates teachers, confuses parents and fails children. Let us make it simple.

What inclusive education is NOT

  • It is not just admitting every child. Admitting a child with a learning difficulty and leaving them to struggle alone at the back is not inclusion. It is abandonment with a nice name.
  • It is not lowering standards. Inclusion means everybody gets the support they need to reach their own best standard.
  • It is not only for children with disabilities. It is for every child: the slow learner, the fast learner, the child who speaks a different language at home, the child from a poor family, the shy child and the gifted child.
  • It is not the job of one teacher alone. It needs the school owner, head teacher, parents and sometimes an outside specialist working together.

What inclusive education really is

Inclusive education means every child learns together, with the support they need to succeed. It rests on a simple belief: every child can learn, every child belongs and every child deserves to be taught in a way that works for them. In practice it means three things.

1. Presence: the child is in the classroom

The child is not hidden at home, sent away or kept in a corner. They are part of the class, the assembly and the school family.

2. Participation: the child is actually learning

Presence is not enough. The child must answer questions, do activities, join group work and make friends. A child who is physically present but mentally lost has not yet been included.

3. Progress: the child is moving forward

Progress is measured from the child's own starting point. For one child it may be writing a full sentence. For another it may be reading a full paragraph. Both matter.

Why inclusive education matters in Nigeria

1. Policy and law support it. Nigeria has a National Policy on Inclusive Education, first approved in 2017 and later revised. The revised policy was ratified by the National Council on Education in December 2023 as a working document, and states are encouraged to adopt it for their own schools. The Child's Rights Act and the Universal Basic Education Act also support every child's right to basic education. Policies and state laws change, so check the current position with your State Ministry of Education.

2. It is practical. In many communities in Rivers State there is no special school nearby. If we do not include children in regular schools, many will not go to school at all. For most families, inclusion is the only realistic option.

3. It helps all children. Research from many countries suggests that well-run inclusive classrooms help every child. Pupils learn patience, kindness, teamwork and respect. Teachers become better teachers because they learn to teach in more than one way.

7 practical steps schools can start today

You do not need a big budget or a new building. You need a change of mindset and a few practical steps.

Step 1: Know your learners

At the start of term, find out who struggles with reading, who cannot see the board well, who needs more time and who learns best by doing. A simple observation sheet for each class can reveal more than any exam. You cannot support a child you do not understand.

Step 2: Teach the same lesson in three ways

Do not teach only by talking and writing on the board. Say it, show it, let them do it. When teaching addition, say "2 + 3 = 5", show it with bottles or stones, then let the children group the stones themselves. The child who did not understand by hearing may understand by doing.

Step 3: Change the seating, change the result

A child who cannot see well should sit in front. A child who is easily distracted should sit away from the window and door. A struggling child should sit near a patient classmate, not at the back alone. Pair struggling learners with peer buddies.

Step 4: Give extra time and different ways to answer

Some children know the answer but cannot write it quickly. Allow oral answers sometimes, give extra time in tests, and split a long test into two shorter parts. This is fair assessment: we are testing what the child knows, not how fast they write. Follow your exam body's rules for formal examinations.

Step 5: Create a simple Individualized Education Plan (IEP)

An IEP does not have to be 20 pages. For a child who needs support, write one page: what the child can do now, one goal for this term, three strategies the teacher will use, and how progress will be checked. Review it each term with the parent. An example goal: "By the end of term, Ada will read 20 common three-letter words without help." That is clear, measurable and achievable.

Step 6: Train and support your teachers

Most teachers in Nigeria were not trained in inclusive education. Do not blame them. Train them. A one-day workshop on spotting learning difficulties, managing a mixed-ability class and simple classroom strategies can change a school. We provide this training with examples from Nigerian classrooms, not only foreign theory.

Step 7: Partner with parents

Parents are partners, not enemies. Tell them what you are doing and give them two or three simple things to do at home. "Your child needs help" can bring shame. "Your child learns differently, and here is how we will help together" brings hope.

What about children with more serious needs?

Some children need more than classroom support. A child who cannot hear or see, or who has a serious intellectual disability, may need a specialist assessment, assistive devices or specialised support for part of the day. Inclusion does not pretend all needs are the same. We start with inclusion in the regular classroom and add more support where needed, whether a learning needs assessment, an IEP or a referral to a specialist. The key is not to send a child away without first trying to understand and support them.

Frequently asked questions

Will inclusive education slow down the bright children?

No. Different methods and peer tutoring help bright children too. They learn leadership, deeper understanding and kindness.

We have 50 children in one class. How can we do inclusion?

Start small. Begin with seating, peer buddies and teaching in three ways. Then add one IEP for the child who needs it most. Small steps done consistently work.

Does inclusion cost a lot of money?

Good inclusion is mostly about mindset and method. Seating, peer support, extra time and teaching aids made from local materials cost almost nothing. Training is the best investment.

What if parents refuse to accept that their child needs support?

Be gentle and avoid labels at first. Show evidence: "Here is what we observed, here is what we tried and here is how your child responded." Offer a confidential assessment. Many parents accept help when they feel respected, not judged.

A final word for school owners and teachers

Inclusive education is not a foreign idea. In our communities we say it takes a village to raise a child. Inclusion is that village inside the school. You do not have to be perfect. Start with one child, one classroom and one change this week. A school that says "Every child belongs here, and we will find a way to teach every child" is a place of hope.
