How To Write a Good Research Project: From Topic to Conclusion

By Obugheni-Polyman Consulting Services · Abua/Odual, Rivers State · For final-year students, postgraduate students and researchers · 9-minute read

Every year, thousands of students in Nigeria reach final year and one fear grips them: "My project."

Some pay someone to write it and then cannot defend it. Some copy from the internet and get caught for plagiarism. Some start well, then get stuck in Chapter 2 for three months. Some abandon it and carry over.

It does not have to be like that. A research project is not a punishment. It is a chance to solve a real problem in your school, community or workplace, and to show that you can think, gather evidence and write clearly.

In our work supporting students and researchers in Rivers State and beyond, the pattern is the same: students who follow a clear step-by-step process finish on time, and students who write without a plan get stuck. This guide takes you from topic to conclusion.

Note: your department's format and your supervisor's instructions always come first. Use this guide to organise your work, not to replace them.

Step 1: Choose a topic that is small, clear and researchable

The biggest mistake is choosing a topic that is too big.

  • Too big: "Education in Nigeria". You cannot cover a whole country in one project.
  • Too vague: "The effect of social media on students". Which students? Which effect? Where?
  • Good: "The effect of social media use on the academic performance of senior secondary school students in Buguma City, Rivers State". Clear, specific, researchable.

Use this formula: Effect or relationship + Who + Where.

Ask three questions before you finalise your topic:

  • Can I get data for this? Can I reach people to interview or give questionnaires to?
  • Is it interesting to me? You will live with this topic for months.
  • Is it useful? Will it help a school, a community or an organisation decide better?

Tip: the best topics are around you: your school, church, market or local government. You do not need to travel to Lagos to do good research.

Step 2: Write your objectives and research questions

Many students skip this and start Chapter 1 immediately. That is why they get lost. Your objectives are your compass.

For the topic above, the main objective is: to examine the effect of social media use on the academic performance of senior secondary school students in Buguma City.

The specific objectives might be:

  1. To find out how often students use social media for academic and non-academic purposes.
  2. To examine the relationship between time spent on social media and academic performance.
  3. To identify strategies that help students use social media positively for learning.

Turn each objective into a research question, for example, "What is the relationship between time spent on social media and academic performance?" If you can answer your questions clearly, you have a good project. If you cannot, your topic is still too vague.

Step 3: Write Chapter 1, the Introduction (the why)

Chapter 1 answers: why are you doing this research? It usually has:

  • Background of the study: three or four paragraphs. Start broad (Nigeria), narrow to Rivers State, then to your school or community. Use real examples.
  • Statement of the problem: what is the problem, why does it matter and what happens if nothing is done? "It is a problem that has eaten deep into the fabric of society" is a slogan, not a problem statement.
  • Objectives, research questions and hypotheses: as in Step 2. Hypotheses are used in quantitative studies.
  • Significance of the study: who benefits, and how?
  • Scope and limitations: be honest. "This study is limited to two schools in Buguma City" is honesty, not weakness.
  • Definition of terms: say what you mean by "social media" and "academic performance" in your study.

Common mistake: copying Chapter 1 from another project. Your supervisor will know. Simple and original is better than big grammar and copied.

Step 4: Write Chapter 2, the Literature Review (what others have said)

This is where most students get stuck. Chapter 2 is not a summary of textbooks. It is a conversation with other researchers: what they found, where they agree, where they disagree and what is still missing. That gap is where your study fits.

Organise it by themes, not by authors.

  • Wrong: "According to Ade (2020)... According to Ngozi (2021)... According to John (2022)..." That is just a list.
  • Right: 2.1 Social media and learning; 2.2 Social media and academic performance; 2.3 Strategies for positive use; 2.4 Summary and gap.

For an undergraduate project, 15 to 25 good sources is usually enough, and 30 to 50 for postgraduate work. Use recent sources (the last 5 to 10 years) plus a few classic ones, from Google Scholar, your library and reputable journals. Avoid blogs and Wikipedia as main sources.

Tip: as you read, record the author, year, what they studied, what they found and what they missed. That table makes Chapter 2 much easier to write. A free tool like Zotero or Mendeley helps you keep your references organised.

Step 5: Write Chapter 3, Methodology (how you will do it)

Chapter 3 answers: how will you get your data? Explain:

  • Research design: survey (quantitative), interviews (qualitative) or both (mixed methods). A questionnaire survey is common for undergraduate projects.
  • Population and sample: who are you studying, how many, and how will you choose them? For example, if the population is 400 SS2 students, the Taro Yamane formula, n = N / (1 + N x e squared), at a 5% margin of error gives a sample of 200. Whatever formula you use, say so and show the working.
  • Instrument: the tool you will use (questionnaire, interview guide, observation checklist) and its sections.
  • Validity and reliability: who checked your questionnaire? Did you test it with about 10 people first?
  • Data collection: how, where and for how long.
  • Data analysis: simple percentages and tables, means, chi-square, or thematic analysis for interviews.
  • Ethical considerations: permission, informed consent, anonymity and safe storage of data.

Key rule: write Chapter 3 so clearly that another student could repeat your study exactly.

Step 6: Collect data and write Chapter 4, Results

Now go to the field. This is the most exciting part.

Rules for data collection:

  • Get permission from the school or organisation first. A letter from your department helps.
  • Explain to respondents that it is for academic purposes and that names will not be published.
  • Never fabricate data. If you are struggling, tell your supervisor early.

Chapter 4 is not about your opinion. It is about what the data says. Present results in tables and simple charts, then explain each in two or three sentences. For example: "Table 4.1 shows that 65% of respondents spend more than three hours daily on social media. This suggests that social media takes a large part of the time students could use for study." Use a heading for each research question and answer them one by one.

Step 7: Write Chapter 5, Discussion, Conclusion and Recommendations

This is where you become a thinker, not just a reporter.

  • Discussion: compare your results with Chapter 2. Did they agree with other researchers? If not, why? This is the most important part, and many students skip it and repeat Chapter 4.
  • Conclusion: summarise your main findings in about one page and answer your research questions directly. No new data.
  • Recommendations: give four or five specific recommendations. Not "Government should do something", but "School owners in Buguma City should keep phones in lockers during lessons, and teachers should use class WhatsApp groups for assignments."
  • Suggestions for further study: one paragraph.

Step 8: References, appendix and defence preparation

  • References: use APA 7th edition unless your school says otherwise, and list only sources you cited.
  • Appendix: your questionnaire or interview guide and the permission letter.
  • Defence: you must explain your project in five minutes without reading. Practise: Why this topic? What did you find? What is your contribution? What would you do differently?

If you wrote your project yourself, you can answer these. If someone else wrote it, you will struggle. That is why we help students to write, not to buy a project they cannot defend.

A note on AI tools and plagiarism

Many schools now use similarity-checking software, and some also set rules on AI tools. Check your school's policy. A safe way to use AI is to help you understand a topic, check grammar or plan your outline, not to produce text you submit as your own. Always write in your own words, cite what you use and never let a tool invent references.

Common mistakes that delay projects

  • Waiting for perfect English before writing anything. Write first in simple English, then improve.
  • Not meeting your supervisor regularly. Meet every two weeks, even with one page.
  • Copying and pasting from the internet.
  • Trying to write all five chapters in one week. Write one chapter at a time.

A simple 10-week timeline

  1. Weeks 1 to 2: topic, objectives and Chapter 1 draft
  2. Weeks 3 to 4: Chapter 2, literature review
  3. Week 5: Chapter 3, methodology and questionnaire
  4. Weeks 6 to 7: data collection
  5. Week 8: Chapter 4, results
  6. Week 9: Chapter 5, discussion and conclusion
  7. Week 10: corrections, references and binding

Ten weeks is achievable if you work by schedule instead of waiting for motivation. Adjust the timeline to your department's deadlines.

Before you submit: a quick checklist

  • My objectives, research questions and conclusion match each other.
  • Every source in the text is in the reference list, and the reverse.
  • My questionnaire and permission letter are in the appendix.
  • My supervisor has seen the full draft.
  • I can explain the whole project without reading.
